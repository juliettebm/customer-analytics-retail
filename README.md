# Customer Analytics on Retail Transaction Data

End-to-end customer analytics on 1,067,371 retail transactions: data quality auditing, relational modelling in SQL, RFM segmentation, cohort retention with survival analysis, repurchase prediction, and a Power BI dashboard.

**Dataset**: [Online Retail II](https://archive.ics.uci.edu/dataset/502/online+retail+ii) (UCI Machine Learning Repository), a UK-based online giftware retailer selling mostly to wholesale buyers, December 2009 to December 2011.

**Stack**: Python, pandas, scikit-learn, lifelines, SQLite, Power BI

## Notebooks

### 01 - Data Preparation

Turns the raw file into an analytical base, with every exclusion inspected against real rows before being applied.

Missing values are measured in revenue as well as in row count, which changes the reading: 22.77% of rows lack a customer identifier but represent only 13.68% of revenue. Cancellations are flagged rather than removed, since the right treatment depends on the analysis. Non-product stock codes are identified from their descriptions, not guessed from a pattern: three codes (`B`, `TEST001`, `TEST002`) escape an alphabetic filter entirely and surfaced only through price anomaly investigation.

**Output**: 820,947 rows, 5,880 customers, £16,728,372 net revenue, 76.9% of raw rows and 86.7% of raw revenue retained.

### 02 - Relational Modelling & SQL Analysis

Decomposes the flat table into four normalised tables with primary keys, foreign keys, a domain constraint and indexes declared in SQLite.

Normalisation requires resolving two conflicts first: 12 customers appear under multiple countries, and 598 stock codes carry multiple descriptions. Both are handled with deterministic tie-breaking rules, documented with their rationale. Integrity is verified before loading and then proved by a deliberately invalid insert that the database rejects.

Analytical queries combine CTEs with four window functions: `RANK` globally and partitioned by country, `NTILE` for deciles, `LAG` for intervals between consecutive orders, and a running total for the concentration curve.

**Output**: `retail.db` plus four CSV extracts feeding the dashboard.

### 03 - Customer Segmentation (RFM)

Builds two segmentations in parallel and uses each to validate the other: quantile-based RFM scoring, the retail operational standard, and k-means clustering, statistically grounded but coarser.

Silhouette analysis selects k = 2 (0.437, well ahead of k = 4 at 0.366). The two methods agree completely at the extremes: Champions falls 100% into the Engaged cluster, Hibernating 100% into Disengaged. Two methods sharing no assumptions cannot agree that completely by chance.

Loyal Customers is the one segment that does not resolve, splitting 882 against 508, which suggests a genuine mixture of two customer types rather than a coherent group.

Stability is tested against the two arbitrary upstream choices: shifting the reference date by 30 days leaves Recency ranking unchanged (correlation 1.0000), and 5.9% of customers change Monetary decile depending on the cancellation-netting rule.

**Output**: `customer_segments.parquet`, 5,838 customers with both segmentations.

### 04 - Cohort Retention & Churn

Builds acquisition cohorts and models time-to-churn with Kaplan-Meier and Cox proportional hazards.

Churn has no observable event in non-contractual retail, so it is defined from the distribution of purchase intervals: 180 days, between the 90th and 95th percentiles. The distribution decays smoothly with no second mode, so any threshold is a stated convention rather than a discovered boundary.

The cohort matrix separates two distinct problems. Cohorts acquired between October and December 2010 start within the normal retention band and then collapse, from 25.9% to 8.8% over four months. Failing to convert a customer and losing one already acquired call for different responses.

The Cox model produces a clean confounding result. First-order value shows a highly significant difference between tiers by log-rank (p < 0.0001), yet no effect at all once purchase frequency enters the model (p = 0.82) or is stratified out (p = 0.19). The tier effect is a proxy for order frequency, not an effect of first-order spend.

**Output**: `customer_survival.parquet`, 5,854 customers.

### 05 - Repurchase Prediction

Classifies whether a customer will order again within 90 days, using only information available before the window opens.

The train/test split is temporal rather than random, since a random split would let the model learn from a customer's own future behaviour. This surfaces a caveat that a random split would have hidden: the positive rate is 32.2% in the training period against 43.6% in the test period, an 11-point gap attributable to the pre-Christmas peak. The test score is therefore optimistic as a year-round estimate.

Logistic regression outperforms the random forest on both metrics (PR-AUC 0.755 against 0.752, ROC-AUC 0.790 against 0.775). The relationship is close enough to linear that added complexity brings nothing, and the simpler model is directly interpretable.

Feature importances are unusually flat, from 0.248 for Recency down to 0.122 for Frequency. No single feature drives the prediction, and Monetary ranks ahead of Frequency, reversing the usual RFM hierarchy.

## Methodological notes

**Leakage control.** Every feature in notebook 05 is computed strictly within the observation window, and the train/test split is temporal. Notebook 01 applies its train/test separation before any feature construction.

**Baselines throughout.** Every model is reported against a naive reference. A PR-AUC of 0.755 means little without the 0.434 baseline beside it.

**Negative results are reported.** First-order value does not predict retention once frequency is controlled for. The threshold optimisation in notebook 05 is degenerate under the assumed costs and is reported as such rather than presented as a recommendation.

**Decisions are logged, not applied silently.** Each cleaning rule, each threshold and each tie-breaking convention is stated with its rationale and its measured impact, so a reader can disagree with a choice and know exactly what it changed.

## Reproducing

```bash
git clone https://github.com/juliettebm/customer-analytics-retail.git
cd customer-analytics-retail
pip install -r requirements.txt
```

Download `online_retail_II.xlsx` from the [UCI repository](https://archive.ics.uci.edu/dataset/502/online+retail+ii) and place it in `data/raw/`.

Run the notebooks in order. Notebook 01 caches the raw Excel file as Parquet on first execution, so subsequent runs take seconds rather than minutes.

The Power BI dashboard reads the CSV extracts written by notebook 02 to `data/processed/powerbi/`.

## Limitations

Guest transactions are out of scope. 22.77% of rows carry no customer identifier and are excluded, so every customer-level metric describes identified customers only, biased towards higher-value repeat buyers.

The churn threshold is a modelling choice. At 180 days it sits between the 90th and 95th percentiles of observed intervals, and every survival figure would shift with a different value.

12.9% of observed purchase intervals are same-day, likely split orders rather than genuine repeat purchases. No field in the data distinguishes them, so they are retained.

The dataset ends on 9 December 2011, so the final month is incomplete and the final cohorts are observed for a few weeks only.
